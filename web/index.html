What the simulator models

Every 100 ms each device sends a small real-time packet (like a voice or control message). The simulator draws its latency from a simplified model of Wi-Fi channel access:

  • Contention. More active devices means more collisions and retries. OFDMA groups devices into resource units, so fewer of them fight for the channel.
  • Busy channel. Bulk transfers hold the channel for long transmissions (up to ~4 ms). A real-time packet waits behind them. Frame preemption cuts that wait.
  • Neighbour networks. Overlapping networks add busy time. Multi-AP coordination (Wi-Fi 8) removes most of it.
  • Multi-link operation. Wi-Fi 7 sends on whichever of two bands frees up first, which trims the latency tail.
  • Scheduled access. Restricted TWT / TSN-style scheduling gives real-time traffic reserved slots, which is what makes Wi-Fi behave most like a cable.
  • Queueing. Without prioritization, real-time packets sit behind bulk data in the AP queue (bufferbloat).

An Ethernet switch is simulated alongside as the "wired" reference. Wired-likeness compares median latency, p99 latency, jitter and loss against it: 100% means indistinguishable from a cable.

This is a teaching model, not a standards-accurate simulator like ns-3. Use it to explore trends, not to quote absolute numbers.

What the live test measures

  • Idle latency and jitter: 30 small HTTP round-trips to the server.
  • Download / upload: 4 parallel streams for 8 s each, ignoring the first second while TCP ramps up.
  • Latency under load: pings while a download saturates the link. The rise over idle latency is your bufferbloat grade.

Run server.py on a computer plugged in by Ethernet, so the Wi-Fi hop is the only wireless part being measured.
